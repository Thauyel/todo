The Anatolian original. Lamb filling, unleavened dough, every dumpling folded
by hand into a tiny open envelope. Served over garlic yogurt with browned butter,
pul biber, and dried sumac.

Serves 4 generously as a main (Kayseri portions are absurd — plan accordingly).

## Dough
- 300 g plain flour (plus extra for dusting)
- 1 large egg
- 100 ml water (approx — adjust)
- 1 tsp salt
- 1 tbsp olive oil

## Filling
- 300 g ground lamb (not too lean — 15–20% fat keeps it tender)
- 1 small onion, very finely grated (or pulsed in a food processor)
- 1 tsp salt
- 1 tsp Turkish pul biber (or any mild red pepper flakes)
- 1/2 tsp black pepper

## To serve
- 500 g plain thick yogurt (full-fat — Balkan-style or Greek-style works)
- 2 cloves garlic, crushed to a paste with 1/2 tsp salt
- 80 g unsalted butter
- 1 tsp pul biber
- 1 tsp dried sumac (essential — this is the Turkish finish)
- 1 tsp dried mint (optional)

## Method — dough
1. Mound the flour on a clean surface or in a wide bowl. Make a well in the center.
2. Crack the egg into the well. Add the salt, olive oil, and half the water.
3. Mix with a fork, drawing flour in from the sides, adding the rest of the water a little at a time until you have a stiff, smooth dough. It should be slightly firmer than pasta dough — it needs to hold a fold without stretching out.
4. Knead 8–10 minutes until the dough is smooth and springs back when poked. Wrap in cling film and rest at room temperature 30 minutes.

## Method — filling
1. Mix the lamb, grated onion, salt, pul biber, and black pepper in a bowl. Knead with your hand for 2–3 minutes — the stickiness is what makes the filling hold together inside the tiny folds.
2. Cover and refrigerate until ready to fold.

## Method — folding (the part that matters)
1. Roll the dough as thinly as you can — about 1 mm thick, almost translucent. Cut into 1.5 cm squares. (A pizza cutter on a floured surface is the fastest tool.)
2. Place a tiny pinch of filling (about 1/4 tsp) in the center of each square.
3. Fold into a triangle: bring two opposite corners together, pinch, then bring the third corner up and pinch all three at the top — you want a tiny open pyramid with the filling peeking through the top.
4. Set each folded mantı on a floured tray. Don't let them touch or they'll stick.
5. Repeat. With practice this takes 3–4 minutes per dozen. A full batch is 80–100 pieces. Kayseri households brag about how many they can fold in an hour. Don't worry about speed on your first attempt — the taste is identical regardless of shape.

## Method — cooking
1. Bring a wide pot of generously salted water to a gentle boil (not a rolling boil — the dumplings are delicate).
2. Drop the mantı in a single layer, in batches if needed. Stir very gently once to prevent sticking.
3. Cook 8–10 minutes. They're done when they float and the dough is tender (bite one — no raw flour taste). Lift out with a slotted spoon into a colander. Drizzle with a few drops of olive oil and toss gently so they don't stick to each other.

## Method — to serve
1. Whisk the crushed garlic into the yogurt. Spread a generous layer across the bottom of a wide, shallow serving dish (or individual plates).
2. Pile the warm mantı on top of the yogurt.
3. Brown the butter in a small pan over medium heat until the milk solids turn golden and it smells like toasted hazelnut. Off the heat, stir in the pul biber — it should sizzle and bloom red.
4. Spoon the spiced butter over the mantı. Shower with sumac and the optional mint.
5. Serve immediately, with more pul biber on the table for the brave.

## Notes
- **Kayseri sizing is a point of pride.** A traditional plate holds 40+ pieces. Don't make them big — the small size is what makes them cook evenly and what makes them feel like an event.
- **The grated onion is non-traditional but practical.** Most Kayseri recipes use very finely chopped onion, but grating distributes moisture more evenly and saves time. The flavor is identical after cooking.
- **Skip the egg in the dough if you want it vegan-style or closer to the Kayseri original** (many recipes use only flour + water + salt). With egg, the dough is easier to work with — your call.
- **Sumac is not optional.** It looks like paprika but tastes sour/fruity — that's the bright top note on the finished dish. Find it at any Middle Eastern grocer.
- **Freeze before cooking.** Arrange raw folded mantı on a floured tray, freeze until solid, then bag. Cook from frozen, adding 2 minutes to the boil time. Weeknight mantı is now 15 minutes.
- **No lamb?** Use beef + a small amount of lamb fat for flavor, or all beef. Don't use chicken — the flavor is too mild for the yogurt-and-butter topping.
- **Leftover mantı** can be pan-fried in butter until the bottoms are crisp and golden. Served with a fried egg on top, this is *kavurmalı mantı* — a legitimate Turkish second meal.
